The village market opens early in the morning. Farmers bring tomatoes, onions, green chillies and bananas on bicycles and in small trucks.
By seven o'clock the street is full of people. Shopkeepers call out their prices and buyers walk from stall to stall, comparing the vegetables.
A tea seller stands near the bus stop. He pours hot tea from one cup to another to cool it, and the smell of ginger fills the air.
Children on their way to school stop to buy biscuits. Their teacher waits at the gate and counts them as they come in.
In the afternoon the market becomes quiet. The farmers count their money, load the empty baskets and start the long ride home.
Learning to program is a little like learning to cook. At first you follow every step exactly. Later you understand why each step is there, and you begin to change the recipe.
A tokenizer breaks text into small pieces called tokens. A model reads tokens, not letters or words, so the way text is split changes how much a model can read at once.
Byte pair encoding starts from single bytes and repeatedly joins the most common pair of neighbouring tokens into a new token. After many merges, common words become one token.
Rare words are still possible, because they can always be built from smaller pieces, down to single bytes.
